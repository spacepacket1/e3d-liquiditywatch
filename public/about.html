What is the U.S. Financial Stress Score?

The methodology behind the number on the LiquidityWatch homepage.

The score answers one question: how close is the U.S. financial system to a point where policymakers are forced to act?

That's a different question than recession probability, VIX, inflation, or Treasury yields taken in isolation. It's closer to proximity × severity × probability of policy-forcing financial-system stress — a judgment call that requires synthesizing Treasury-market mechanics, funding-market plumbing, and how the Fed and Treasury are actually behaving, not just what a single indicator says.

How it's produced

Each evaluation runs through three AI stages, each a different model, so no single model's judgment silently becomes the answer:

  • Research & scoring — a deep-research model does live web research across Treasury, funding-market, and Fed-policy sources and proposes a score.
  • Independent critique — a second model reviews that evidence independently, without seeing the first model's number, and derives its own. A real disagreement between the two triggers a targeted follow-up search on the specific point in dispute.
  • Narrative synthesis — a third model writes the plain-language summary, explicitly explaining any disagreement between the first two rather than quietly averaging it away.
Every evaluation — material or not — is stored. Only evaluations judged materially different from the last published one trigger a new score, a notification, and a newsletter issue. No daily noise.

Scale and regime

The score runs 0–100: 0–15 accommodative, 16–35 mild watchfulness, 36–55 real but contained tension, 56–75 persistent restrictive policy (active QT at multi-year-high yields with a genuinely undecided upcoming Fed decision already belongs here, even with no crisis signal — restrictive policy plus real two-sided uncertainty isn't calm), 76–90 policy-forcing danger zone, 91–100 market dysfunction or intervention underway. A second, related figure — how much Treasury/Fed policy has actually begun supplying liquidity — is tracked alongside it, since high stress with a rising policy response reads very differently than high stress with no response at all.

Trigger metrics

Alongside the headline score, each evaluation tracks three metrics explicitly against the last published one — not reasserted fresh every cycle, and each shown as a percentage: Controlled Break Risk (the odds current stress breaks into an uncontrolled crisis rather than staying policy-managed), Liquidity Response Probability (the odds the Fed/Treasury actually supplies new support soon — distinct from how much support is already underway), and Phase (1 = tightening/policy-threat, 2 = liquidity response beginning, 3 = full backstop underway). Each carries its own stated reason for holding or moving, and, when the trend is meaningful, a note on whether it's accelerating or slowing. BTC, ETH, and XRP each get a short trigger note plus a score reflecting how much that asset is actually functioning as settlement/bridge liquidity (XRP) or confirming the broader liquidity regime (BTC/ETH) right now, not just price momentum — XRP additionally gets a bridge-role read (adjacent asset through core routing asset) and whether stablecoin flow is actually routing through it or bypassing it.

Sub-engine breakdown

The headline score is a synthesis of six domains tracked individually, each its own evidence-backed read: Treasury Market Stress, Funding Market Stress, Fed/Treasury Response, AI Economic Impulse (with a disinflationary/neutral/inflationary classification), Energy System Stress, and Global Liquidity. When populated, these appear as their own panel — useful for seeing which domain is actually driving a move in the headline number, not just that it moved.

Facts, interpretation & speculation

Where present, each evaluation's supporting detail is split into three explicitly labeled tiers: observed facts (measurements, each with a source), interpretation (what those facts imply — the model's judgment), and speculation (possibilities beyond what the facts currently establish). Speculation is always labeled as the lowest-confidence tier regardless of its own stated confidence number — the label, not the number, is what governs how much weight to put on it.

Policy classifier & falsifiable calls

Alongside the numeric band, each evaluation is classified by observed policymaker behavior — not the score alone — into one of five states: Normal, Stress Building, Controlled Stabilization, Systemic Backstop, or Monetary Regime Change. Where present, a call also states one specific, checkable claim about what a real Fed/Treasury response would look like and by when — the basis for eventually scoring whether published calls were actually followed by what they predicted.

The AI council & “What Could Happen Next”

After a material evaluation, a small council of AI roles looks at that day's data and the score's last 30 days and writes up to four scenarios for the next 30 days: one where stress keeps falling, one where it rebounds, one tail-risk break, and one where nothing changes. The roles are run through E3D Pilot, each on a model from a different AI company, so no single model's view is the whole story:

  • Macro Desk — reads the data for what it implies and through which channel.
  • Skeptic — challenges the other roles.
  • Fact-Checker — checks the figures and says which can't be verified, so unverified claims are dropped.

These are AI roles, not people. Each scenario says if something checkable happens by a stated date, then which part of the score should move and in which direction, what it is backed by, what it depends on, what would prove it wrong, and rough odds (low, medium or high, never a precise probability). Where the roles genuinely disagree, the disagreement is shown.

Checked by code, not by a person. Every scenario must pass an automatic check before it is published: it needs a dated, checkable trigger; it must name the part of the score it affects and a direction, and never a target score; the facts it cites must come from the data supplied to the council; and it can't read as investment advice. If fewer than three scenarios pass, nothing is published rather than a weaker version. On the page, the outlook shown is the latest one that passed, with its date.

A track record, graded automatically. Each scenario carries a machine-readable test against LiquidityWatch's own published numbers. After its date passes, code — not a model — grades it as came true, did not, or could not be checked, and later outlooks report how past scenarios did. Scenarios are graded on how the stress score and its components moved, not on market prices.

Limits. Scenarios are a way to think about what could change the picture, not predictions, and the odds are rough. A role may object to the exercise or to a specific scenario; that is part of the point of having one.

Fully automated

This is an information product, not a trading system or an advisory service, and there is no human-approval step in the publishing path — a material evaluation publishes automatically the moment the three-stage process completes, and the “What Could Happen Next” scenarios publish the same way, with the automatic check above standing in for a human reviewer.

Where the data comes from

Live web research plus a FRED-sourced macro grounding context (Treasury yields, a broad-dollar proxy, high-yield credit spreads) supplied to the research stage on every cycle.
